Feynman's Tuva

Throat singing and quantum physics

Richard Feynman spent the last decade of his life trying to reach Tuva, and died two days before the invitation arrived. What reached him instead was a voice that sings two notes at once. The man, the country, the music, and an instrument built from the physics they share.

One · Summer 1977

Whatever happened to Tannu Tuva?

A dinner in Pasadena, a stamp album from the 1930s, and a capital city spelled with too many Ys.

In the summer of 1977 Richard Feynman was at dinner with Ralph Leighton, a schoolteacher and drummer who had become his closest friend, and Leighton made the mistake of claiming to know every country in the world. Feynman had a question ready. When he was a boy in Far Rockaway his father had shown him a country on a map, a purple shape in the middle of the great green mass of Asia, and its stamps were the best things in his album: triangles and diamonds printed with camels, reindeer and horsemen, from a place called Tannu Tuva. Then it stopped appearing anywhere. So: whatever happened to Tannu Tuva?

Leighton assumed it was a joke. They got the encyclopaedia down. Tuva was real. It had been an independent republic from 1921 to 1944, and it had then been absorbed into the Soviet Union, where it sat on the Mongolian border, about as far from any ocean as it is possible to be. Then they saw the name of its capital.

“K-Y-Z-Y-L. My wife and I and he at the same time looked at each other and grinned, because any place that's got a capital named Kyzyl has just got to be interesting.”Richard Feynman, The Quest for Tannu Tuva, 1988

They shook hands on it. They would go. It took a while to understand how hard that would be. The library had shelves of accounts of travel through central Siberia, and none of the travellers had gone through Tuva. It was a bowl ringed by mountains, Feynman said, and everybody went around it. The one book they found had been published in Berlin in 1931 by Otto Mänchen-Helfen, the first non-Russian to set foot in the republic, who reported that the Kyzyl power station only ran while the cinema was showing a film, and that the audience, shown a close-up, shouted that they had paid full price and wanted to see the whole person. Feynman read that and decided they had discovered Shangri-La.

Two · 1918 to 1988

The physicist

Who he was, in the years the letters were going unanswered.

Feynman was born in Queens in 1918, took his doctorate at Princeton in 1942 under John Wheeler, and spent the war at Los Alamos in Hans Bethe's theoretical division, where he ran the computing group, opened his colleagues' safes for sport, and watched the Trinity test through a truck windscreen because he had worked out that glass would stop the ultraviolet. At Cornell after the war he rebuilt quantum electrodynamics, the theory of how light and electrons interact, which at the time gave infinite answers to finite questions.

His fix came with two tools that physics still uses every day. The first was the path integral. Rather than following a particle along one route, you add up a contribution from every route it could possibly take and let the contributions interfere. The second was the diagram. A Feynman diagram is a cartoon of an interaction, straight lines for electrons and a wiggle for a photon, and every cartoon stands for an exact term in the calculation. In 1965 he shared the Nobel Prize with Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga for that work. He told the Horizon crew that the prize had been a pain in the neck.

By then he was at Caltech, where he stayed for the rest of his life. The course he gave to first-year students from 1961 to 1963 became The Feynman Lectures on Physics, and the third volume, the quantum one, opens by saying that the double-slit experiment contains the only mystery, then spends the rest of the book on what follows from it. In 1959 he had given a talk called “There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom” that is usually counted as the beginning of nanotechnology. In May 1981, four years into the Tuva project, he gave another, “Simulating Physics with Computers”, which argued that an ordinary computer can never efficiently imitate a quantum system, so you would have to build a computer out of quantum parts. “Nature isn't classical, dammit,” the published version says, “and if you want to make a simulation of nature, you'd better make it quantum mechanical.” Every quantum computer now being built descends from that sentence.

He also played the drums, and not as a hobby that got a line in the obituary. He and Leighton were once hired in San Francisco to write and perform a percussion score for a ballet. The ballet went to a competition in Paris and came second, and when the choreographer asked the judges what had let it down, they said the music. “So we were found out,” Feynman told the camera, pleased. “In Paris, at last, somebody had enough cultural background to recognise the fakers.”

Three · 1977 to 1988

Ten years of letters

A phrasebook, a licence plate, a radio programme, and a plan involving an art exhibition.

The first replies were from Intourist, the Soviet travel agency, and the answer was a form. A scholar of Tuvan studies wrote back more kindly to say that Leighton's interest alone doubled the number of people in the field, and that the obstacles were, in his opinion, insurmountable. Feynman's plan was to be invited to lecture in Moscow and make Tuva a condition. Leighton's was to learn Tuvan.

Intourist, Moscow

Dear Mr Leighton, we have received your letter requesting us to arrange a visit to Tuvinskaya. We regret to inform you that the subject area is beyond Intourist travel routes. Therefore we cannot arrange such a visit. Sincerely.

They got hold of a Tuvan–Russian phrasebook and a dictionary and built letters out of them, changing the printed phrases as little as they dared so that the mistakes would be few. Greetings to your country from ours. We have a great interest in Tuva. We hope one day to visit. If there is any way you could send us recordings of the language we would be grateful. They posted it, Feynman said, “into the vacuum, as usual”. Then one day Leighton came running over waving an envelope he had not dared to open. It had come from Kyzyl. It was in Tuvan. They worked through it word by word, from the phrasebook into Russian and from Russian into English.

Ondar Daryma, Kyzyl

Greetings from Tuva. Kyzyl is a nice city. You will find we have a monument to the centre of Asia.

“That was the beginning of hope,” Feynman said. It had taken years.

Along the way the country came to them in pieces. A Los Angeles radio station played a record that a Russian ethnographer had posted to them, and the presenter warned listeners that what they were about to hear was one man, not a man and a flute. That was the first throat singing either of them had heard. A team of botanists got into Tuva to count leaf buds, which was maddening but instructive: you needed official business. Leighton found a throat-singing conference in western Mongolia, close to the border, and they signed up as delegates, imagining Tuvans who would carry them home in their wagons. Then the Mongolian minister of culture changed and the conference was cancelled.

In January 1986 the Challenger broke apart, and Feynman was asked to join the commission investigating it. He did not want to go. His wife Gweneth told him that eleven of the commissioners would move around in a knot and one would run everywhere like a mosquito, and that if there was anything strange to find, he would find it. He went, and on television he dropped a piece of the rocket's O-ring seal into a glass of ice water to show that it lost its spring in the cold. He wrote home from Washington.

Richard Feynman, Washington, 1986

Dearest Gweneth and Michelle, this is the first time I have had time to write to you. This is an adventure as good as some of the others in my book. I already smell certain rats that I will not forget, because I just love the smell of rats, for it is the spoor of exciting adventure. Tuva or Bust! Love, Richard.

The route that finally opened was an art exhibition. Leighton learned of a Soviet show of nomadic art with Tuvan objects in it and set about getting it to Los Angeles, on the understanding that whoever brought it over would have to travel to the places the objects came from. When the museum asked what his finder's fee was, he said there was none. The fee was Tuva. The Soviet delegation who came ahead of the exhibition understood the trick at once, since two thirds of their own protocol was gimmicks for getting Russians to Disneyland, and the senior man, delighted by an evening among Feynman's parrot-owning, music-making friends, said: you should come to Tuva, and I am going to guarantee it.

Moments
Four · February 1988

Two days

Three days after that evening Feynman went into hospital. He had been living with cancer for ten years, since a tumour the size of a football was cut out of his abdomen in 1978, and a second, rarer cancer of the blood had followed. He died at the UCLA Medical Center on Monday 15 February 1988, aged 69. Two days later a letter arrived from Moscow.

Academy of Sciences of the USSR, Moscow

Dear Professor Feynman, I have the great pleasure to invite you, your wife and four of your colleagues to visit the Soviet Union. I was informed by Professor Kapitsa that you would like to visit Tuva and get acquainted with its sights. We consider the most favourable time for such a trip to be the period of May and June 1988. Your trip will take three to four weeks. Kindly note that the Academy of Sciences will cover your expenses.

Yours sincerely, Vice President Velikhov.

Leighton had kept a secret for the day they reached the monument: T-shirts printed with a map of Tuva, a shirt that said Kyzyl with a heart, souvenir hats reading Tuva, Centre of Asia. “The chief never knew about it,” he says in the film, and then, asked what happens to the project now, he stops being able to speak. Feynman, in the last interview, had already answered the question of what it had all been for.

“It's a game. It's not serious. It doesn't involve some deep philosophical point of view about authorities or anything. It's just the fun of having an adventure, to try to go to a land that we never heard of.”Richard Feynman, the last interview, 1988

The film ends with him at the drums. Leighton went to Kyzyl that year and stood at the centre of Asia without him. The exhibition, Nomads: Masters of the Eurasian Steppe, opened at the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County in November 1989. Feynman's daughter Michelle made the trip in June 2009. On 11 May 2018, his hundredth birthday, Kyzyl held a celebration on the banks of the Yenisei, with Leighton, with physicists, with throat singers, and with a ceremony at the monument. By then the country he never reached had spent thirty years sending its music out into the world along the road he had opened.

“It was the adventure with my friend that this was all about.”Ralph Leighton, 1988
Five · Khöömei

The music that came back

One voice, several pitches, and the physics of a singer's mouth.

Tuvan throat singing, khöömei, is a family of techniques for making one voice sound like two. The singer holds a low drone, and above it a second voice appears, a clear whistle that moves in a melody of its own while the drone stays put. There is no trick and no second person. Every sung note is already a stack of harmonics: the fundamental plus its whole-number multiples, two times, three times, four times the frequency and on up. Your ear normally fuses them into one note with a colour. The vocal tract shapes that colour. It is a tube with resonances, and moving the tongue and lips moves the resonances, which is how vowels work. A khöömei singer narrows the tract until one resonance becomes so sharp that it lets a single harmonic through and starves the rest. That harmonic, usually somewhere between the sixth and the thirteenth, is heard as a separate note. To play a melody the singer hops from one harmonic to the next, which means the melody is made of whole numbers: 8, then 9, then 10, then 12. The scale of a sygyt tune is the harmonic series.

There are three main styles and many small ones. Khöömei proper is soft and mid-range. Sygyt, the word means whistle, pushes the fundamental higher and gets the piercing, flute-like overtone. Kargyraa is the deep one. The singer brings the vestibular folds, the false vocal cords above the true ones, into vibration as well, and they lock in at exactly half the frequency, so a second fundamental appears an octave below. Chylandyk combines sygyt and kargyraa. Singers traditionally went out to the steppe and the rivers to do it, where the open country carries the sound a long way, and the styles are named for the things they imitate: wind over the grass, water over stones, the gait of a horse.

For a long time the route from Kyzyl to the wider world ran through Leighton's living room. After Feynman's death he founded Friends of Tuva, and in 1993 the society brought Kongar-ool Ondar to California. Ondar, born in 1962 by the Khemchik river, was already Tuva's most celebrated singer, a People's Khöömeizhi of the republic. At a reception in San Francisco a blind blues musician called Paul Pena, who had heard throat singing on a shortwave radio in 1984 and taught himself kargyraa from a record, stood up in the crowd and sang back at him. Two years later Friends of Tuva sent Pena to Kyzyl for the khöömei symposium, where he won the kargyraa prize, and the film of that journey, Genghis Blues, won the audience award at Sundance in 1999 and was nominated for an Oscar.

The same year Ondar released Back Tuva Future on Warner Bros., a record that put throat singing next to Willie Nelson and Randy Scruggs and, on two tracks, next to Richard Feynman. Leighton had tapes of Feynman drumming and chanting, and on “Tuva Groove” and “Kargyraa Rap” the drums under Ondar's voice are his, and so is the voice you hear talking. Feynman is credited as a co-writer. A hidden track at the end runs excerpts from his lectures with no music under them at all. He never heard a Tuvan sing in a room with him. The record put him in one.

Six · The allegory

What they have in common

The ladder, the sum, and where the likeness stops.

1Only certain waves fit

A string fixed at both ends can only vibrate in shapes that fit between the ends: one hump, two, three. Its frequencies are whole-number multiples of the lowest. The buzz of the vocal folds is the same kind of ladder. In 1913 Niels Bohr explained why atoms only emit certain colours with the same rule: an electron's wave has to fit a whole number of wavelengths around its orbit, so only certain orbits, and only certain energies, exist. Schrödinger's equation of 1926 made that exact. It is a wave equation, and its bound solutions are standing waves. “Quantised” means only certain modes fit. A throat singer's ladder of overtones and an atom's ladder of levels are quantised for the same reason, and the first view of the instrument below draws the two side by side.

2A state is a sum

Any sound is a sum of harmonics with weights: this much of the fundamental, that much of the eighth. That is Fourier's theorem, and it is exactly the form a quantum state takes, a sum of modes with weights, which is what superposition means. When a khöömei singer reshapes the mouth, they are adjusting the weights, preparing a state. A pure sygyt whistle is one weight dominating all the others, as close to a single mode as a voice gets.

3The ladder Feynman built his career on

The energy levels of a particle trapped in a box are not equally spaced; they go as one, four, nine. The levels of a quantum harmonic oscillator are equally spaced, like overtones. And in the quantum electrodynamics Feynman shared the Nobel Prize for, the electromagnetic field is a collection of harmonic oscillators, one for every colour of light, and a photon is one rung up the ladder. Two photons, two rungs. A sygyt singer stepping from the eighth harmonic to the ninth is stepping up a ladder with the same spacing as the one light climbs.

4Feynman's arrows

In QED, the 1985 book he wrote for readers with no mathematics, he explains the whole theory with little arrows. Every path a photon could take gets an arrow that turns like a stopwatch hand as the photon travels; you add the arrows tip to tail, and the length of the result, squared, is the probability. A Fourier sum is the same arithmetic. Each harmonic is an arrow turning n times as fast as the first. Add them tip to tail and the tip traces the singer's waveform. The third view of the instrument draws exactly that, with the sixteen measured harmonics as its sixteen hands.

Where it stops

The weights of a sound are real numbers, and you hear all of them at once. The weights of a quantum state are complex numbers, and their squares are the odds of what you would find if you looked; look, and you find one answer. No one's ear collapses a drone. The likeness is the ladder and the sum. The difference is what the sum means, and the instrument is built to keep that difference in view rather than lose it in the glow. It is a picture of a voice, drawn in the language of a state.

Seven · In honour of Feynman

The instrument

A quantum visualiser that listens to throat singing.

It finds the drone, measures the first sixteen harmonics, and turns them into the weights of a sum of modes. Then it draws that sum three ways: as standing waves in a box beside their ladder, as Bohr's waves fitting around an orbit, and as Feynman's arrows added tip to tail. Play one of the recordings, or give it your own voice: hold a low note and slide the vowel slowly from ee to oo, and watch the rungs light in turn.
