Feynman's Tuva

Throat singing and quantum physics

Richard Feynman rebuilt the quantum theory of light, then spent the last decade of his life trying to reach Tuva. He died two days before the invitation came. What reached him instead was a voice that sings two notes at once, and it turns out to work the way his physics does.

One · Summer 1977

Whatever happened to Tannu Tuva?

At dinner in 1977 Feynman's friend Ralph Leighton claimed to know every country in the world. Feynman had a question ready. As a boy his stamp album had held triangles and diamonds printed with camels and reindeer from a place called Tannu Tuva, a purple shape in the middle of Asia, and then it had vanished from every map. They looked it up. Tuva was real, swallowed by the Soviet Union in 1944, and its capital was spelled K-Y-Z-Y-L.

“Any place that's got a capital named Kyzyl has just got to be interesting.”Richard Feynman, The Quest for Tannu Tuva, 1988
Two · The quantum physicist

The man who drew the calculation

By the time he asked the question, Feynman had already changed what physics looks like. In the 1940s the quantum theory of light and electrons gave nonsense answers to simple questions. He fixed it with two ideas that are now everywhere. Add up every path a particle could take, and let the paths interfere. Draw each interaction as a cartoon, a line for an electron and a wiggle for a photon, where the cartoon is the calculation. That won him the 1965 Nobel Prize. In 1981, four years into the Tuva project, he gave the talk that started quantum computing. Nature isn't classical, he said, so if you want to simulate it you had better build the simulator out of quantum parts.

Three · 1977 to 1988

Ten years of letters

Intourist said Tuva was beyond its travel routes. So they taught themselves Tuvan from a phrasebook, built a letter out of its printed phrases, and posted it, as Feynman put it, into the vacuum. Years later an envelope came back from Kyzyl, in Tuvan, from a man called Ondar Daryma.

Ondar Daryma, Kyzyl

Greetings from Tuva. Kyzyl is a nice city. You will find we have a monument to the centre of Asia.

After that the country came to them in pieces: a record of throat singing played on Los Angeles radio, a licence plate that read TOUVA, a cancelled conference in Mongolia, a letter home from the Challenger inquiry signed “Tuva or Bust! Love, Richard.” The door that finally opened was an exhibition of nomadic art that Leighton talked into coming to Los Angeles, with no finder's fee. The fee was Tuva.

Four · February 1988

Two days

Feynman had lived with cancer for ten years. He died in Los Angeles on Monday 15 February 1988, aged 69. Two days later a letter arrived from Moscow.

Academy of Sciences of the USSR, Moscow

Dear Professor Feynman, I have the great pleasure to invite you, your wife and four of your colleagues to visit the Soviet Union. I was informed by Professor Kapitsa that you would like to visit Tuva. We consider the most favourable time for such a trip to be May and June 1988. Kindly note that the Academy of Sciences will cover your expenses.

“It's a game. It's not serious. It's just the fun of having an adventure, to try to go to a land that we never heard of.”Richard Feynman, his last interview, 1988

Leighton went to Kyzyl that year and stood at the centre of Asia without him. Michelle Feynman made the trip in 2009. In 2018, on his hundredth birthday, Kyzyl celebrated him on the banks of the Yenisei with throat singers.

Five · Khöömei

One voice, two notes

Every sung note is already a stack of overtones, whole-number multiples of the note you hear. A Tuvan singer holds a low drone, then shapes the mouth until one overtone rings out on its own as a second, whistling voice. To play a melody the singer hops from one overtone to the next: the eighth, the ninth, the tenth. The tune is made of whole numbers. Hold that thought.

Six · 1999

Feynman on the record

He never heard a Tuvan sing in a room with him. Back Tuva Future put him in one.

After Feynman's death Leighton founded Friends of Tuva, and in 1993 it brought Kongar-ool Ondar, Tuva's most celebrated singer, to California. In 1999 Ondar released Back Tuva Future on Warner Bros., with Willie Nelson, Randy Scruggs, and, on two tracks, Richard Feynman: Leighton's tapes of him drumming, chanting and talking, mixed under Ondar's voice. Feynman is credited as a co-writer. A hidden track runs his lectures with no music at all.

Seven · The quantum in the voice

What they have in common

Four ideas, and the place where the likeness stops.

1

Only certain waves fit

A string can only vibrate in shapes that fit between its ends, so its notes come in whole-number steps. Bohr's atom obeys the same rule: an electron's wave has to fit around its orbit a whole number of times, so only certain energies exist. “Quantised” means only certain modes fit. A throat singer's ladder of overtones is quantised for the same reason an atom's ladder of levels is.

2

A state is a sum

Any sound is a sum of overtones with weights: this much of the drone, that much of the eighth. A quantum state has exactly that form, a sum of modes with weights, which is all superposition means. When the singer reshapes the mouth, they are adjusting the weights. A pure whistle is one weight winning: as close to a single mode as a voice gets.

3

The ladder light climbs

In Feynman's quantum electrodynamics every colour of light is an oscillator with equally spaced rungs, and a photon is one rung. Two photons, two rungs. A singer stepping from the eighth overtone to the ninth is climbing a ladder with the same spacing as the one light climbs.

4

Feynman's arrows

In QED he explained the whole theory with little arrows that turn like stopwatch hands and add tip to tail. A voice is the same arithmetic: each overtone is an arrow turning n times as fast as the first, and the tip of the chain draws the singer's waveform. The instrument below draws exactly that.

Where it stops

The weights of a sound are plain numbers, and you hear all of them at once. The weights of a quantum state are the odds of what you would find if you looked, and looking gives one answer. Nobody's ear collapses a drone. The likeness is the ladder and the sum. The difference is what the sum means, and the instrument keeps that difference in view rather than losing it in the glow.
