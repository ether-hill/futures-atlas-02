Quantum Superposition Visuals

A quantum state written as a sum is not a blurred object waiting to sharpen up. It is a list of what could be found, with a weight on each. The picture worth having is of the options, all of them, laid out at once.

Nature is full of processes that draw exactly that and nothing more: a fungus spreading into every corridor, a river dividing until it runs out of water, a spark testing every site on its rim, a crack too fast to stay single, a flock that has not settled. None of these is a superposition. Each one is a possibility space you can watch being made, and each panel says which half of the idea it is carrying and which half it is not.

Five of them, running live. Nothing loops, because nothing here repeats: these grow, so the control that matters is the seed. Reseed any panel and you get another run of the same rules.

The honest version, stated once so no panel has to keep apologising: none of these five is a quantum superposition, and the page is not claiming that any of them is. A river really is in all of its channels, a fungus really is in all of its corridors, and neither is mysterious. What they share with the quantum picture is one half of it — the possibility space, the set of routes a process could take, drawn all at once so its shape is visible. They are a picture of the option set. They are not a picture of a fuzzy object, and reading them as one is the error this page exists to avoid.

Nothing here loops. Every field is a growth process with a history rather than a period, so there is no timeline to scrub and no clip that closes seamlessly. Every field is also stochastic, which is why each panel carries a seed: reseeding gives you another run of the same rules, and the same seed gives you the same run back.

The models are the standard ones and are named in each panel. Breakdown is the dielectric-breakdown model with the growth exponent stated; the flock is Reynolds boids; the delta and the fracture are simplified to what reads at this size, with the physics they simplify described rather than implied. Where a panel names a real effect, the effect is real and the picture is an illustration of it.
